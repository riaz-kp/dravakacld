Impact story

The estuary that stopped feeding the reef plastic

Aghanashini estuary, Karnataka·Three seasons

For two years we cleaned the same beach at Kaup and got roughly the same weight back every month. That is the frustrating part of shoreline work — if the source is upstream, you are mopping with the tap running.

So we followed it up. Most of what we were pulling off the sand was matching the debris caught in the estuary mouth 11 km north. We put a net-and-boom trap at the mouth with two panchayats, cleared it fortnightly, and ran a segregation session for the three villages feeding into it.

The beach haul at Kaup dropped by just under half in one season, and stayed down the next. The trap is now maintained by the panchayat, not by us, which is the only version of this that lasts.

Why it matters: plastic intercepted in an estuary is still in one piece. The same plastic recovered from open water three months later is fragments, and the fragments are what end up inside fish.

Impact story

Ninety-two Saturdays

All six bases·Monthly since inception

Nobody writes an article about a beach clean-up. They are early, unglamorous and exactly the same every month. That repetition is the entire point.

Ninety-two of them so far, 18.4 tonnes off the sand and the seabed. But the number we watch is the composition, not the weight. In the first year, 61% of what we lifted was single-use packaging. Last season it was 38%, with fishing gear now the largest share — a different problem, needing different conversations.

The other change is who shows up. The first clean-up had eleven people, nine of them ours. The last one had 140, and we ran out of gloves.

Why it matters: a debris line sitting on a dune stops the grass rooting. Clear it consistently and the dune stabilises, which is the difference between a beach and a sand pit that moves inland every monsoon.

Impact story

2,600 mangroves later, the creek bank held

Aghanashini creek·Year three

The stretch of bank we started on had been losing about a metre a year. The village had moved a footpath twice. Planting mangrove there was not a carbon project — it was an attempt to stop somebody’s land walking into the water.

We raised saplings in a nursery rather than transplanting wild stock, planted with the panchayat over three seasons, and went back every six months to count what was still alive. 71% at three years, which is well above what unmonitored planting usually returns.

Last monsoon the bank did not move. That is one season and one site, and we are not going to pretend it is proof. But the footpath is where it was.

Why it matters: mangrove holds sediment, filters run-off before it reaches the reef, locks carbon at several times the rate of inland forest, and shelters the juveniles of most of the fish we dive with.

Impact story

340 fragments, and what we learned from the ones that died

Netrani & Kaup nurseries·18 months

A coral nursery is a rope or a frame, a set of cable ties and a lot of patience. You collect fragments that have already broken off — from storms, anchors, careless fins — and give them somewhere to attach instead of rolling on sand until they suffocate.

340 fragments in, 210 grown out and returned to the reef. Branching Acropora did best, and did noticeably better on the shallower frames where light is stronger and the surge keeps them clean. The massive Porites we tried early on mostly failed; they grow too slowly for this method to be worth the maintenance dives.

So we stopped using them. Publishing that is more useful than publishing only the 210.

Why it matters: reef structure is habitat. Lose the structure and you lose the fish, the grazers that keep algae down, and eventually the tourism that pays the coastal towns.
