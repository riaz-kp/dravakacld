Help centre

Questions, answered properly.

Booking and safety first, then the bigger questions people ask us about the environment. We have tried to answer those honestly rather than comfortably.

Booking & trips

Do I have to pay when I book?

No. We confirm your place first, then send a payment link for a 25% deposit with the balance due on the day. Courses over ₹50,000 can be split. Card details are never entered on this site.

What if the weather cancels my dive?

You get a full refund or a free reschedule, your choice. We would rather lose the booking than put a boat out in conditions we do not like.

Can I dive if I cannot swim well?

For a try dive or snorkelling, yes — you need to be comfortable in water, not fast. Certification courses require a 200 m swim with no time limit and a 10-minute float.

Is there an age limit?

Try dives from 10, DSD and Open Water from 12, free diving from 16, Divemaster from 18. Under-18s need a guardian signature on the liability form.

What medical conditions stop me diving?

Asthma, heart conditions, epilepsy, recent surgery, ear problems and pregnancy all need a doctor’s clearance before we can take you. Declare it at booking — it is much better than being turned away on the morning.

What is included in the price?

Certification fees, all gear hire, boat charges and the guide or instructor. Not included: travel, accommodation unless stated, medical clearance, and personal insurance.

Membership & volunteering

Do I need to be a member to volunteer?

No. Clean-ups and planting weekends are open to anyone. Membership funds the year-round work and gets you priority on trips, but it is not a gate.

Does membership auto-renew?

Only if you ask it to. We email you a month before expiry either way.

Can my school or company join a clean-up?

Yes, and we would like that. Tell us numbers two weeks ahead so we can bring enough gloves and bags. Get in touch.

Donations

Where does my donation actually go?

Entirely to the mission side: clean-ups, mangrove and coral nurseries, and the education programme. Admin is capped at 7.5%. The breakdown and the receipts are published each season.

Can I cancel a monthly donation?

Any time, from the link in any receipt email or by writing to foundation@dravaka.example. No retention call.

Will I get a receipt?

Yes, by email, immediately. It includes a short note on what your gift is funding this season.

About the environment

The questions we get asked most often on trips, in schools, and by people deciding whether any of this is worth their Saturday.

What is the best way to keep the earth clean?

Stop waste at the point it is created, rather than chasing it afterwards. In practice that means three things, in descending order of usefulness:

  • Refuse and reduce. Every item of single-use packaging you do not accept is one that never needs collecting, sorting or burning.
  • Segregate at home. Mixed waste is nearly worthless to a recycler and usually ends up in a pit or a fire. Separated waste has a value, which means someone has a reason to collect it.
  • Do the physical work locally and regularly. One person clearing the same stretch every month achieves more than a thousand people doing it once for a photograph.

Everything else — the campaigns, the pledges, the hashtags — only matters if it produces one of those three.

Why do I have to help the environment?

Because you are inside it, not next to it. The practical answer is that the systems people treat as scenery are also infrastructure. Mangrove is a sea wall. Reef is a fishery and a storm buffer. Dune grass is what stops a beach walking into a village. When they fail, the replacement is concrete, and concrete costs orders of magnitude more and works less well.

The honest answer is also that nobody is coming to do it for you. There is no authority with the budget, the staff and the local knowledge to look after every kilometre of coast. What exists instead is people who happen to be there. If you swim, dive, fish or walk somewhere regularly, you are the person who is there.

What does “save the environment” actually mean?

Taken literally it means very little, which is part of the problem — it is broad enough to feel important and vague enough to require nothing.

A usable definition: keeping natural systems functioning well enough to keep providing what people and other species depend on — clean water, breathable air, stable coastlines, soil that grows things, and populations of animals large enough to survive a bad year.

Note what that is not. It is not returning anywhere to a pre-human state, which is neither possible nor what anyone actually wants. It is maintenance of a working system, and maintenance is measurable. That is why we publish tonnage, survival rates and out-planted fragments instead of adjectives.

What are the advantages and drawbacks of protecting the environment?

Pretending there are no costs is how these arguments get lost, so:

Advantages. Coastal protection that repairs itself for free. Fisheries that keep producing. Tourism income that continues rather than exhausting itself. Cleaner water and air, with the health costs that follow. Carbon locked into mangrove and seagrass at rates that beat almost anything inland. And the plain fact that a functioning coast is a better place to live.

Drawbacks. They are real and they usually land on the people with the least room to absorb them. Restrictions on fishing or sand extraction hit livelihoods now, while the benefit arrives in five years. Protected areas can push activity to somewhere less regulated rather than stopping it. Conservation work costs money that could have gone elsewhere. And badly designed rules — written far from the coast, enforced unevenly — can generate resentment that outlasts whatever they protected.

Which is why our version is local, voluntary, and paid for by the people doing it. Nobody near our bases loses income because of a Dravaka clean-up. If protection cannot be made worth someone’s while, it does not hold once the attention moves on.

How do I clean my environment?

Concretely, starting this month:

  • Pick one place. A beach, a lake edge, a stretch of road. Somewhere you already go. Familiarity is what makes it repeatable.
  • Go monthly, not once. Put it in a calendar. The first visit is the least useful one.
  • Sort what you collect. Glass, metal, PET and mixed. Find out who takes each locally before you start — if you do not know where it goes, it goes nowhere good.
  • Weigh it and write it down. Sounds fussy. It is the only way to know if anything is changing, and the only thing that will convince anyone else to join you.
  • Follow it upstream. If the same debris keeps arriving, the source is somewhere else. Find it, and talk to whoever lives there.
  • Take one other person. This is the step that turns a hobby into something that outlasts you.
